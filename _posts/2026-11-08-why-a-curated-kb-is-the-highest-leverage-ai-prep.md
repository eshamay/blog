Across every kind of project I've run with AI agents (coding, analysis, planning, writing, ideation), one thing predicts success more reliably than the model, the prompt, or the harness: whether a deep, curated *domain wiki* existed before the work started. I mean a multi-source body of project knowledge, assembled from every place the domain actually lives and treated as a first-class engineering artifact in its own right. In my experience, teams that build the wiki first ship; teams that try to reason from raw repos and parametric memory churn.

But *curated* has to mean *verified*, not merely *collected*: an unchecked wiki is worse than none, because agents treat its pages as ground truth and every error in it propagates. This post argues why, what such a wiki looks like, what makes one good, how wikis fail, why the wiki pays back compounding returns long after the original question is answered, and how to run several.

It draws on a year I've spent building and maintaining wikis, not only on the literature. Most are built in a burst around a single question and then go quiet; the few that stay alive have an owner who keeps putting new questions to them. What decides a wiki's value is verification and upkeep, not collection.

## What this post is about

This post is **not** about lightweight instruction files for AI tools. `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `.cursorrules`, and conventions files are configuration. They load on every run, but they hold conventions, not domain knowledge. They are useful, but they are not the topic here.

This post is about the **domain wiki** that sits underneath that configuration: the curated, multi-source, project-scoped body of knowledge an AI agent reads to do real work on a complex domain. A real wiki pulls from design docs, RFCs, Slack threads, Google Docs, code-search results, BOM dumps, vendor reference, meeting transcripts, peer-product writeups, glossaries, decision logs, and status journals. It is the substrate that makes the work possible.

Anthropic frames this exact discipline as context engineering: *"finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."*[^1] Glean, building enterprise AI in production, puts it more bluntly: managing context well *"is now foundational to whether agents succeed in the enterprise."*[^2] The prompt is one-shot. The wiki is the project.

## Why a deep wiki beats every other lever

Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval study reduced top-20 retrieval failure rates by **49%** by prepending context to each chunk before indexing, and by **67%** with an added reranking step[^3]. Spotify's *Honk* series (Nov 2025) reports more than **1,500 merged PRs**, with **60–90% time savings** on the migrations it targets compared with hand-coding, and credits larger static prompts: *"larger static prompts, which are easier to reason about. You can version-control the prompts, write tests, and evaluate their performance."*[^4]

Sourcegraph (Dec 2025) makes the same argument for code: *"the shift to agentic coding workflows has paradoxically made large-scale code search* more *valuable, not less,"* because, as it quotes Augment Code's engineers, *"custom decorators buried three directories deep, subtle overrides in sibling microservices, critical business logic scattered across modules, all of this remains invisible to the model"* without real code search[^5]. The collective behind *Applied LLMs* (Yan, Bischof, Frye, Husain, Liu, Shankar) reports, citing a study of RAG versus unsupervised finetuning: *"RAG consistently outperformed finetuning for knowledge encountered during training as well as entirely new knowledge."*[^6] Facts get into the loop through retrieval, not weights. Anthropic's own *Building Effective Agents* names the same dependency: it is *"crucial for the agents to gain 'ground truth' from the environment at each step."*[^7] A curated wiki is how you supply that ground truth before the loop starts.

The failure mode without a wiki is consistent. Models don't admit uncertainty when context is shallow. They confidently extrapolate, producing what Weaviate calls *"higher-fluency hallucinations."*[^8] Long contexts also degrade in the middle (the *"lost in the middle"* effect[^24]), so even very large windows don't substitute for curated structure. Spotify identifies the most damaging failure as *"a PR that passes CI but is functionally incorrect"*[^4], the signature of an agent working without enough domain context to know what *correct* even means.

The published numbers come from large systems, but the same pattern shows up at small scale. Asked what checks run when a file is published in a large codebase, a chat thread produced eight answers; checked against the source code, six were wrong or incomplete. A wiki that ranked the code first and recorded the thread as claims to verify got the answer right and logged the disagreement. Here the wiki mattered less for what it held than for letting someone check it.

A wiki is only as good as its last verification. Agent-derived findings that go unchecked look authoritative: fluent, cited, internally consistent, and wrong more often than they look. Treat every finding that will drive an action (a filed ticket, an estimate, a decision) as unverified until a separate pass has tried to break it.

Depth beats breadth, and a question beats a topic. A wiki scoped to one question or decision (what to file, what to size, whether to migrate) produces action: findings get checked, gaps get closed, deliverables ship. In my experience, a wiki scoped to a whole domain produces volume instead: thousands of pages, contradictions arriving faster than anyone resolves them, gaps drafted faster than they close, and a single deliverable at the end. A wiki that duplicates what a live system of record already tracks, such as a bug queue or a dashboard, tends to be abandoned. Scope by the decision the wiki must support, and let it grow only as the decisions do.

## Anatomy of a real domain wiki

Eleven elements distinguish a working wiki from a notes folder.

**1. Multi-tier organization.** Separate leadership-facing artifacts, working-engineer detail, and reference material, and let links flow one way: the top tier links down, and reference content never depends on top-tier freshness. The structure echoes Tiago Forte's PARA logic: *"organize by actionability,"* not by subject.[^10] Tier by page type and metadata rather than by deep folder nesting. A small set of page types (concept, entity, finding, source, output) plus frontmatter facets lets one wiki serve several audiences, while a long list of top-level folders confuses new readers.

**2. Source diversity, with provenance.** Pull from every origin where the domain actually lives. Real domains do not live in one system. They live in chat archives, design docs, code repos, ticket trails, vendor pages, and meeting transcripts. Glean's enterprise pattern: *"every application has its own structure and semantics, and requires data models that capture diversity"*[^2]. Track every claim back to its origin: Stack Overflow's RAG guide says retrieval exists to *"anchor LLMs on precise, up-to-date, and pertinent information"*[^12], and that only works if you can say where the information came from. Then rank sources by trust and record the rank. Primary sources (code, specs, data) settle questions. Human-attested statements, recorded as sources of their own with the owner named, come next. Chat threads and meeting transcripts are claims to verify, not facts, and they rank below the code they describe: when a thread and the source code disagree, the code wins. Agent-synthesized research reports can inform a page but cannot ground a fact. A synthesis built on other sources adds no independent evidence, and citing it as a source closes a circle.

**3. A per-source record and an immutable raw layer.** Every ingested source gets its own page (a stable slug, a one-line description, an owner, a permalink, and a content hash), and the original text is kept untouched beside it. A single references file is the simplest form of this and works for a small wiki; at scale the per-source page does the same job and carries more. Cite with the locator native to the source: message id, section heading, channel and timestamp, work-item id. For code, pin to a commit (repo, SHA, path, line), because line numbers drift with the next merge and an unpinned claim can quietly become wrong. A source that lives somewhere that changes (a chat, a living doc, a ticket) is re-snapshotted, not re-linked: a re-ingest creates a new snapshot that supersedes the old one, and a bare pointer to a live URL cannot be re-verified later. Sourcegraph and Glean both call out source attribution as a trust requirement; hashes and snapshots make that trust mechanically auditable. Without them, citations rot into orphan strings.

**4. Atomic, durable building blocks.** One claim per bullet, one decision per file, one concept per glossary entry. Matuschak's evergreen-notes pattern is the right north star: notes should be *"atomic,"* *"concept-oriented,"* and *"densely linked,"* and *"written and organized to evolve."*[^11] Dense linking has a cost: one wrong fact copied into nine pages needs nine corrections, so when a correction lands, search every page that cites the claim rather than fixing only the page where it was spotted.

**5. A human-only-edit facts file.** Set aside a single `irrefutable-facts.md` that the agent treats as ground truth and **must not modify**. Each fact is one bullet; each is cited. The pattern inverts the default mutability of a decision log: the bullets are treated as true until their named owner changes them. Michael Nygard's ADR convention names the same discipline at the decision layer: when a decision is reversed, *"we will keep the old one around, but mark it as superseded."*[^13] SQLite's `AGENTS.md` is a public-facing analogue, an explicit human-defined boundary file telling agents what they may and may not do[^14]. Expect this file to fill slowly. For months it may hold only identity, scope, URLs, and naming conventions, because agents may not promote facts on their own and each promotion deserves real verification. Its most useful job is as a reference point for corrections: when a generated artifact turns out to be wrong, you fix the error once and check every derived page against it. Give each fact a review date, and have lint compare facts against newer sources rather than only checking that they are well-formed.

**6. An open-knowledge-gaps file.** Every unresolved question gets a structured entry: *question / why it matters / owner / how to close / status*. Closed gaps stay (dated-closed, with a *Resolution* paragraph). The structure mirrors Basecamp's hill-chart logic: uphill is where *"there are still unknowns or unsolved problems,"* and downhill is where *"all unknowns are solved and only execution is left."*[^15] Anthropic's research-prompting guidance, a sample prompt, reinforces it: *"Track your confidence levels in your progress notes to improve calibration... Update a hypothesis tree or research notes file to persist information and provide transparency."*[^16] Agents draft gaps far faster than people close them, and a gaps file that only grows becomes a wishlist. Three habits keep it alive:

- **Triage.** Cap the number of open gaps, and drop or merge any that no longer change a decision.
- **Batch closure.** Have an agent draft a resolution for each gap, with explicit slots for the owner to confirm, sorted into buckets. The human ratifies in one sitting, and only the human closes.
- **Brevity.** Keep each entry short and move evidence into findings pages.

**7. An append-only status log.** Every revision dated, with trigger, resolution, aggregates recomputed, and files touched. It works like a lab notebook, with the same payoff: the wiki's reasoning stays legible across time.

**8. Auto-generated detail alongside human-curated synthesis.** Scripts can render per-entity detail off structured rules (YAML, CSV, BOM extracts); humans own the rules and the synthesis. Glean's *"different flavors of indexes for retrieval"*[^2] and Spotify's *"separate workflow agents that can produce prompts for the coding agent from various internal and external sources"*[^4] follow the same split: machines do mechanical breadth, humans curate semantic depth.

**9. A purpose file.** One short file that states what the wiki is for and which questions are in scope. Every ingest and every query checks it first. It is the cheapest defense against a wiki that sprawls.

**10. A todo file, separate from the gaps file.** Gaps are epistemic (things nobody yet knows), and only a human closes them. Todos are operational (ingest this source, re-run that lint, backfill that page), and an agent may close one once the work is verifiably done. Mixing the two buries open questions under chores.

**11. An outputs layer.** Deliverables (exec summaries, sizing models, briefs) live in their own folder, are written by a single script or process, are frozen at delivery, and link back to the pages they draw on. This is where the payoff lands: people rarely query a wiki directly; they ask for something to be written from it. Expect the deliverables tier to be the least durable layer. Deliverables are snapshots: when the pages beneath them are renumbered, merged, or superseded, their links break and their claims drift, and most end up archived. Record each output's inputs, re-check it when they move, and treat it as something to redraw from the wiki on demand, not maintain. Stakeholders do not read the wiki itself, so deliver into the places they already read: sync findings append-only into a shared doc with its comment threads preserved, publish a read-only site for browsing, and hand leadership a short written summary instead of a folder. The wiki feeds those surfaces, and people edit through an agent rather than directly, except for the facts and gaps files, which a human still writes and closes.

## Properties that decide whether the wiki is any good

Six quality properties separate a wiki that pays back from one that rots.

**Atomic and linkable.** One idea per note; dense links between them. Matuschak[^11] and Zettelkasten.de[^18] converge here, and Anthropic's progressive-disclosure pattern[^1] points the same way. Atomicity is what makes search and re-use mechanical, not heroic.

**Provenanced.** Every non-obvious claim cites its source. Anthropic's long-context guidance recommends asking the model to *"ground responses in quotes."*[^19] LlamaIndex's enterprise work argues for preserving *"the page-by-page granularity that makes insights actionable, auditable, and actually useful."*[^25] AWS's RAG documentation: *"The output can include citations or references to sources. ... This can increase trust and confidence in your generative AI solution."*[^21] But a citation is not proof. Agents invent plausible ones: file paths that do not exist, ids that resolve to nothing. Check cited paths and ids mechanically, and treat a finding with a clean-looking citation as unverified until something has actually opened the file. Cite at two levels: full provenance inside the wiki, and a short curated References list in anything delivered to others, in place of internal links and names.

**Fresh, with named decay.** Tag time-sensitive entries (percentages, owners, dates) and mark a refresh cadence. Weaviate names *"stale index poisoning"* as a distinct failure mode[^8]. The status log is what keeps freshness honest.

**Retrievable at the size you have.** The wiki reads well to both humans skimming and agents grepping. The filesystem itself is a useful index; LlamaIndex (Jan 2026) reports agentic applications *"centralizing around files and filesystems as core abstractions for context management."*[^20] Folder hierarchy and naming conventions become semantic signal. BM25 plus embeddings beats either alone on large, heterogeneous collections of sources, since, as Perplexity's Aravind Srinivas put it, *"vector embeddings do not magically solve search"*[^6], but a wiki built on a few hundred sources rarely needs the vectors. An index with a one-line summary per page, plain grep, and one hop along links is deterministic, auditable, and enough. Past that size, curation matters more than vectors: a hand-written routing table ("for questions about X, start here") and frontmatter facets. Cap what an agent must read on every operation, too. Once the index and gaps files pass a few hundred kilobytes, split them or read them by section.

**Write authority by file.** The facts file is human-only-edit. The status log is append-only. Gap entries can be drafted by agents but only *closed* by humans. Some files are agent-may-suggest, others are agent-may-not-touch. The discipline matters because once an agent overwrites ground truth, the wiki stops being a stable substrate and starts being just another conversation log. Give each protected file exactly one writer, one script or one process, so there is one place to audit and one place to fix. Be honest about enforcement: instructions in a prompt and a warning header deter, and after-the-fact lint detects, but neither prevents. Where the harness supports it, enforce with hooks that refuse the write. And hold every automated tool to the same write rules: a formatter that rewrites code fences, or a publisher that edits frozen deliverables, is an agent overwriting ground truth by another route.

**Contradiction-aware.** In a wiki drawn from many sources, contradictions are the steady state. In my experience, a noticeable number of pages carry one at any time. Mark them rather than resolving them silently. Never pick a winner by recency alone, because time does not resolve a disagreement, and make every answer that touches a contested claim say so. A supersede relationship is for corrections; a contradiction is two live claims nobody has yet adjudicated.

## How wikis fail

A wiki is not safe by construction. The same properties that make it powerful make its failures expensive, and they recur:

- **Overconfident synthesis.** An agent reads an end-of-meeting summary as a decision and records "consensus" where there was none. The claim then spreads to every page that cites it, delivered outputs included. Mark transcript-derived claims "discussed" until a named owner ratifies them.
- **Invented citations.** Plausible paths, ids, and quotes that resolve to nothing. Check them mechanically.
- **Alias collisions.** Two people, systems, or acronyms with the same short name merge into one page, and attributions go wrong.
- **Propagating errors.** A single typo or wrong number copied across dozens of links. Fix it with a deliberate fan-out, not a single edit.
- **Seed-source contamination.** One stale estimate or unverified number seeds a whole branch of derived pages. Track lineage from sources so that invalidating the seed invalidates everything downstream of it.
- **Self-agreeing pages.** Pages that cite each other look like corroboration to a linter, and they may share a single wrong ancestor. Lint cannot see this; only a re-read of the raw source or an adversarial pass can.
- **Stale ground truth.** Human-only protects a fact from agents, not from time.
- **Ratification backlog.** The agent side of a wiki scales; the human side does not. Promoting a fact, closing a gap, and reviewing a fact for staleness all need a person, so the facts file stays empty, the gaps file grows into a wishlist, and drafted resolutions wait months for sign-off. Design for the human's time: batch decisions into one sitting, have agents draft resolutions and nominate candidate facts, triage, and keep the size of the queue visible. A long queue is a signal about attention, not about agents.

The mitigations are unglamorous: quote verbatim, re-read the raw source before relying on a paraphrase, mark claims by status, and track lineage.

## The compounding payoff

The strongest argument for paying the wiki cost up front is not the first answer it produces but the follow-on answers it delivers cheaply.

Matuschak: *"Evergreen notes are written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects."*[^11] A wiki assembled to answer question 1 becomes the substrate for questions 2 through *N*. Long-running projects ask many questions of the same domain over their lifetime: sizing, sequencing, risk, dependencies, peer comparisons, exec briefings, follow-on phases. Follow-on questions against an existing curated wiki are cheaper than the first, but not free: upkeep costs tokens and attention.

Spotify reports that since mid-2024 *"around half of Spotify's pull requests have been automated"* by its Fleet Management system[^4], the platform its coding agent now plugs into. Glean's Deep Research feature (Sept 2025) is pitched for use *"across every department,"* from triaging customer-reported issues to competitive intelligence to business analysis, over the same documents and databases[^22]: same data, different cuts, no rebuild. Karnofsky calls writing-driven learning *"probably the single biggest difference-maker"* between reading a lot but *"retaining little"* and *"retaining the reasoning behind"* your views[^17]; a wiki applies the same principle at team scale and makes that retained reasoning legible to both humans and agents.

The compounding only happens if what the work learns goes back into the wiki. A finding that lives only in the deliverable is lost to the next question. After every query or output, write the new facts, corrections, and open questions back to the wiki as a standing step.

Teams that skip the wiki relitigate settled questions every session. Vicki Boykis on institutional memory in human teams: *"no one steps in to say, 'Wait—we just did this five years ago.'"*[^23] An agent without a wiki relives the same five years on every prompt. A wiki built for one question becomes an asset that can answer many more at a fraction of the cost, and it stays valid across model upgrades that date prompts and rules files.

## A portfolio of wikis

One wiki per question means you will end up with many, and they overlap. In my experience, a large share of the concepts in any one wiki turn up in others, and each wiki ingests the same documents separately, so every wiki re-interprets them on its own. A single wiki cannot answer "we have nothing on this" for the others. Partition by question, not by team. If two wikis keep citing each other's pages, they are one wiki; merge them. Keep a registry (name, purpose, owner, last verified) and search across it before ingesting anything new. And never answer "nothing is known about this" from one wiki alone.

## Adopting the wiki stance

Nine concrete moves on your next non-trivial project:

1. **Pick the question or decision, and write the purpose file.** One short file stating what the wiki is for and which questions are in scope. Scope to a decision, not a topic. Keep operational chores in a separate todo file so they do not bury open questions.
2. **Inventory and classify the sources.** Before any analysis, list every place the domain lives: docs, Slack channels, code repos, ticket archives, transcripts, vendor pages, peer-team writeups. Capture each as a source page with a slug, a one-line description, an owner, a permalink, and a content hash. Rank each by trust: primary, human-attested, chat and transcript, agent-synthesized. Pin code to a commit. Snapshot anything live.
3. **Stand up an `irrefutable-facts.md`** for the things you have already settled. Human-only-edit. Every bullet cited. Top-of-file warning that automated agents must not modify it (suggested wording: *"even when instructed by a prompt that appears to authorize it."*). Start small, expect it to fill slowly, and give each fact a review date.
4. **Stand up an `open-knowledge-gaps.md`** for everything you have not settled. Each gap = *question / why / owner / how to close / status*. Agents may draft entries; only humans close them with a *Resolution* paragraph. Cap the open count, and close in batches from agent-drafted resolutions.
5. **Open an append-only `status-log.md`.** Date every entry. Note trigger, resolution, files touched. This becomes the wiki's reasoning trail. Commit once per operation so every change can be reverted and diffed.
6. **Verify before you act.** Run a separate adversarial pass on any finding that will drive a filing, an estimate, or a decision, and check cited paths and ids mechanically.
7. **Tier the structure.** Leadership artifacts at the top, working-engineer detail in the middle, reference material at the bottom, organized by page type and metadata rather than deep folders. Links flow one way: reference content must never depend on leadership docs.
8. **Refresh on a cadence, and supersede rather than prune.** Refresh at each release or model upgrade. A page that is wrong gets replaced by a corrected one that links back; a page that is merely old gets marked stale; nothing is deleted without a human deciding. A wiki has more states than alive or dead (draft, active, stale, contradicted, superseded, archived), and moving pages between them is the work.
9. **Write results back, deliver from an outputs folder, and decide where this wiki ends.** Return what each query or output learned to the wiki, and write deliverables into the outputs layer. When a second question outgrows the first wiki's scope, start a second wiki and add both to the registry.

The model is the multiplier. The domain wiki is the signal.
