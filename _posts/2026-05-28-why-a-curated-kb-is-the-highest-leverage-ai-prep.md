Across every kind of project I've run with AI agents — coding, analysis, planning, writing, ideation — one thing predicts success more reliably than the model, the prompt, or the harness: whether a deep, curated *domain corpus* existed before the work started. Not a rules file. Not a CLAUDE.md. Not a `.cursorrules`. A *corpus*: a multi-source body of project knowledge assembled from every place the domain actually lives, and treated as a first-class engineering artifact in its own right. Teams that build the corpus first ship; teams that try to reason from raw repos and parametric memory churn. But *curated* has to mean *verified*, not merely *collected*: an unchecked corpus is worse than none, because agents treat its pages as ground truth and every error in it propagates. This piece argues why, what such a corpus looks like, what makes one good, how corpora fail, and why the corpus pays back compounding returns long after the original question is answered.

## What this doc is — and is not — about

This doc is **not** about lightweight instruction files for AI tools. `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `.cursorrules`, conventions files — those are configuration. They tell an agent how to behave on a one-shot run. They are useful, but they are not the topic here.

This doc is about the **domain corpus** that sits underneath that configuration: the curated, multi-source, project-scoped body of knowledge an AI agent reads to do real work on a complex domain. A real corpus pulls from design docs, RFCs, Slack threads, Google Docs, code-search results, BOM dumps, vendor reference, meeting transcripts, peer-product writeups, glossaries, decision logs, and status journals. It is not a side effect of the work — it is the substrate that makes the work possible.

Anthropic frames this exact discipline as context engineering: *"finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."*[^1] Glean, building enterprise AI in production, puts it more bluntly: *"managing context well is now foundational to whether agents succeed in the enterprise."*[^2] The prompt is one-shot. The corpus is the project.

What follows draws on a year of building and maintaining corpora since this piece was first written, not only on the literature. Most corpora are built in a burst around a single question and then go quiet; the few that stay alive have an owner who keeps putting new questions to them. Reuse shows up as new deliverables more than as repeat queries. And the work that decides a corpus's value is verification and upkeep, not collection.

## Why a deep corpus beats every other lever

The case is increasingly quantified.

Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval study reduced top-20 retrieval failure rates by **49–67%** purely by changing how the corpus was prepared — same model, same query, only the chunk-level context shape changed[^3]. Spotify's *Honk* series (Nov 2025) reports more than **1,500 merged PRs across hundreds of repos**, with **60–90% time savings** vs. hand-coding, and explicitly attributes the win to corpus-shaped inputs: *"larger static prompts, which are easier to reason about. You can version-control the prompts, write tests, and evaluate their performance."*[^4] Sourcegraph (Dec 2025) makes the codebase-as-corpus argument plain: *"the shift to agentic coding workflows has paradoxically made large-scale code search* more *valuable, not less,"* because *"custom decorators buried three directories deep, subtle overrides in sibling microservices, critical business logic scattered across modules, all of this remains invisible to the model"* without a real corpus[^5]. The collective behind *Applied LLMs* (Yan, Bischof, Frye, Husain, Liu, Shankar) puts the architectural punchline cleanly: *"RAG consistently outperforms unsupervised fine-tuning on both familiar and entirely new knowledge."*[^6] Facts get into the loop through corpora, not weights. Anthropic's own *Building Effective Agents* names the same dependency: *"agents need ground truth from the environment at each step."*[^7] A curated corpus is how you supply that ground truth before the loop starts.

The failure mode without a corpus is consistent. Models don't admit uncertainty when context is shallow — they confidently extrapolate, producing what Weaviate calls *"higher-fluency hallucinations."*[^8] Long context windows decay past the *"lost in the middle"* threshold described in Weaviate's Engram research[^9], so even very large windows don't substitute for curated structure. Spotify identifies the most damaging failure as *"a PR that passes CI but is functionally incorrect"*[^4] — the signature of an agent working without enough domain context to know what *correct* even means.

The published numbers come from large systems, but the same pattern shows up at small scale. Asked what checks run when a file is published, a chat thread produced eight answers; checked against the source code, six were wrong or incomplete. A corpus that ranked the code first and recorded the thread as claims to verify got the answer right and logged the disagreement. Findings audited before filing, and a cost method re-run on a second case (both below), tell the same story: in each, the corpus mattered less for what it held than for letting someone check it.

The model is the multiplier; the corpus is the signal. A bigger multiplier on shallow input still produces shallow output.

The corpus is only the signal while it is verified. Agent-derived findings that go unchecked look authoritative — fluent, cited, internally consistent — and they are wrong more often than they look. In one audit of seventeen such findings, made before anyone acted on them, nearly a third were refuted outright and about a third more needed corrections or a narrower claim. Treat every finding that will drive an action — a filed ticket, an estimate, a decision — as unverified until a separate pass has tried to break it.

Depth beats breadth, and a question beats a topic. A corpus scoped to one question or decision — what to file, what to size, whether to migrate — produces action: findings get checked, gaps get closed, deliverables ship. A corpus scoped to a whole domain produces volume instead: well over a thousand pages, contradictions arriving faster than anyone resolves them, gaps drafted faster than they close, and a single deliverable at the end. A corpus that duplicates what a live system of record already tracks, such as a bug queue or a dashboard, is abandoned within weeks. Scope by the decision the corpus must support, and let it grow only as the decisions do.

## Anatomy of a real domain corpus

Eleven elements distinguish a working corpus from a notes folder.

**1. Multi-tier organization.** Separate folders for leadership-facing artifacts, working-engineer detail, and reference material. Asymmetric link flow — top tier links down; reference content does not depend on top-tier freshness. The structure echoes Tiago Forte's PARA logic of *"organize by actionability, not by subject"*[^10] and Andy Matuschak's preference for *"associative ontologies over hierarchical taxonomies."*[^11] In practice, tier by page type and metadata rather than by deep folder nesting. A small set of page types (concept, entity, finding, source, output) plus frontmatter facets such as track and layer lets one corpus serve several audiences, while a long list of top-level folders confuses new readers. Expect the leadership tier to be the least durable layer. Deliverables are snapshots: when the pages beneath them are renumbered, merged, or superseded, their links break and their claims drift, and most end up archived. Treat them as things to redraw from the corpus on demand, not as artifacts to maintain.

**2. Source diversity, with provenance.** Pull from every origin where the domain actually lives. Real domains do not live in one wiki — they live in chat archives, design docs, code repos, ticket trails, vendor pages, and meeting transcripts. Glean's enterprise pattern: *"every application has its own structure and semantics, and requires data models that capture diversity."*[^2] Track every claim back to its origin. Stack Overflow's RAG guide captures the principle: *"anchoring LLMs on precise, up-to-date, and pertinent information."*[^12] Then rank sources by trust and record the rank. Primary sources — code, specs, data — settle questions. Human-attested statements, recorded as sources of their own with the owner named, come next. Chat threads and meeting transcripts are claims to verify, not facts, and they rank below the code they describe; when a thread and the source code disagree, the code wins, and answers drawn from chat are often wrong or incomplete. Agent-synthesized research reports can inform a page but cannot ground a fact: a synthesis built on other sources adds no independent evidence, and citing it as a source closes a circle.

**3. A per-source record and an immutable raw layer.** Every ingested source gets its own page — a stable slug, a one-line description, a permalink, and a content hash — and the original text is kept untouched beside it. A single references file is the simplest form of this and works for a small corpus; at scale the per-source page does the same job and carries more. Cite with the locator native to the source: message id, section heading, channel and timestamp, work-item id. For code, pin to a commit — repo, SHA, path, line — because line numbers drift within a day and an unpinned claim can be overturned by the next merge. A source that lives somewhere that changes (a chat, a living doc, a ticket) is re-snapshotted, not re-linked: a re-ingest creates a new snapshot that supersedes the old one, and a bare pointer to a live URL cannot be re-verified later. Sourcegraph and Glean both call out source attribution as a trust gate; hashes and snapshots make that trust mechanically auditable. Without them, citations rot into orphan strings within months.

**4. Atomic, durable building blocks.** One claim per bullet, one decision per file, one concept per glossary entry. Matuschak's evergreen-notes pattern is the right north star: *"atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked, designed to evolve."*[^11] Atomicity is what makes the corpus reusable for the *next* question — not just the one in front of you. Dense linking has a cost: one wrong fact copied into nine pages needs nine corrections, and a misremembered meeting outcome can reach delivered outputs before anyone notices. When a correction lands, fan it out deliberately — search every page that cites the claim — rather than fixing only the page where it was spotted.

**5. Human-only-edit ground-truth files.** Carve out a single file the agent treats as ground truth and **must not modify**. Each fact is one bullet; each is cited. The pattern inverts the default mutability of a decision log: assertions-treated-as-axioms until the named owner edits. Michael Nygard's ADR convention names the same discipline at the decision layer: *"don't alter existing information; supersede."*[^13] SQLite's `AGENTS.md` is a public-facing analogue — an explicit human-defined boundary file telling agents what they may and may not do[^14]. Expect this file to fill slowly. It is normal for it to hold only identity, scope, URLs, and naming conventions for months: agents may not promote facts on their own, and each promotion deserves real verification. Its most useful job is as a correction anchor — when a generated artifact turns out to be wrong, the file lets you fix the error once and check every derived page against it. Human-only does not mean correct. Facts go stale like anything else, so give each one a review date, and have lint compare facts against newer sources rather than only checking that they are well-formed. To speed the fill rate, let agents nominate candidate facts on request and ratify them in batches; a human still writes the bullet.

**6. An open-knowledge-gaps file.** Every unresolved question gets a structured entry: *question / why it matters / owner / how to close / status*. Closed gaps stay (dated-closed, with a *Resolution* paragraph). The structure mirrors Basecamp's Hill Chart logic: *"uphill = unsolved problems remain, downhill = unknowns resolved, only execution left."*[^15] Anthropic's research-prompting guidance reinforces it: *"track confidence levels in progress notes to improve calibration; update a hypothesis tree."*[^16] Agents draft gaps far faster than people close them, and a gaps file that only grows becomes a wishlist. Three habits keep it alive. Triage: cap the number of open gaps, and drop or merge any that no longer change a decision. Batch closure: have an agent draft a resolution for each gap, with explicit slots for the owner to confirm, sorted into buckets, and let the human ratify in one sitting; only the human closes. Brevity: keep each entry short and move evidence into findings pages, because a gaps file that swells past a few hundred kilobytes costs a read on every operation.

**7. An append-only status log.** Every revision dated, with trigger, resolution, aggregates recomputed, and files touched. Same shape as a lab notebook; same payoff. Holden Karnofsky on writing-as-thinking: *"the single biggest difference-maker between 'reading a ton about lots of things, but retaining little' and 'efficiently developing a set of views.'"*[^17] The status log is where the corpus's reasoning becomes legible across time.

**8. Auto-generated detail alongside human-curated synthesis.** Scripts can render per-entity detail off structured rules (YAML, CSV, BOM extracts); humans own the rules and the synthesis. Glean's *"specialized indexes for different data types"*[^2] and Spotify's *"workflow agents pre-generate prompts from internal/external sources"*[^4] are the same shape: machines do mechanical breadth, humans curate semantic depth.

**9. A purpose file.** One short file that states what the corpus is for and which questions are in scope. Every ingest and every query checks it first. It is the cheapest defense against a corpus that sprawls.

**10. A todo file, separate from the gaps file.** Gaps are epistemic — things nobody yet knows — and only a human closes them. Todos are operational — ingest this source, re-run that lint, backfill that page — and an agent may close one once the work is verifiably done. Mixing the two buries open questions under chores.

**11. An outputs layer.** Deliverables — exec summaries, sizing models, briefs — live in their own folder, are written by a single skill or process, are frozen at delivery, and link back to the pages they draw on. This is where the payoff lands: people rarely query a corpus directly; they ask for something to be written from it. Because an output is a snapshot, it goes stale the moment its inputs change, so record the inputs and re-check the output when they move.

## Properties that decide whether the corpus is any good

Six quality properties separate a corpus that pays back from one that rots.

**Atomic and linkable.** One idea per note; dense links between them. Matuschak[^11], Zettelkasten.de[^18], and Anthropic's progressive-disclosure pattern[^1] converge here. Atomicity is what makes search and re-use mechanical, not heroic.

**Provenanced.** Every non-obvious claim cites its source. Anthropic's long-context guidance recommends asking the model to *"ground responses in quotes."*[^19] LlamaIndex's enterprise work argues for page-level granularity *"to enable auditable corpus retrieval with source citations."*[^20] AWS's RAG documentation: *"returning citations alongside answers lets users verify claims and increase trust."*[^21] But a citation is not proof. Agents invent plausible ones — file paths that do not exist, ids that resolve to nothing — so check cited paths and ids mechanically, and treat a finding with a clean-looking citation as unverified until something has actually opened the file. Cite at two levels: full provenance inside the corpus, and a short curated References list in anything delivered to others, in place of internal links and names.

**Fresh, with named decay.** Tag time-sensitive entries (percentages, owners, dates) and mark a refresh cadence. The Applied LLMs collective recommends *"daily review of inputs/outputs … codify failures immediately."*[^6] Weaviate names *"stale index poisoning"* as a distinct failure mode[^8]. The status log is what keeps freshness honest.

**Retrievable at the size you have.** The corpus reads well to both humans skimming and agents grepping. The filesystem itself is a useful index; LlamaIndex (Jan 2026) reports agents *"converging on files and filesystems as their primary interface for context management."*[^20] Folder hierarchy and naming conventions become semantic signal. BM25 plus embeddings beats either alone on large, heterogeneous corpora — *"vector embeddings do not magically solve search"*[^6] — but a corpus of a few hundred sources rarely needs the vectors. An index with a one-line summary per page, plain grep, and one hop along links is deterministic, auditable, and enough. What carries a corpus past that point is curation: a hand-written routing table ("for questions about X, start here") and frontmatter facets such as track and layer. Budget the reads, too. A rule like "read every anchor file before every operation" stops working once the index and gaps files pass a few hundred kilobytes, so set a size at which you split them or read them by section.

**Write authority by file.** Facts files are human-only-edit. The status log is append-only. Gap entries can be drafted by agents but only *closed* by humans. Some files are agent-may-suggest, others are agent-may-not-touch. The discipline matters because once an agent overwrites ground truth, the corpus stops being a stable substrate and starts being just another conversation log. Give each protected file exactly one writer — one skill or one process — so there is one place to audit and one place to fix. Be honest about enforcement: instructions in a prompt and a warning header deter, and after-the-fact lint detects, but neither prevents. Where the harness supports it, enforce with hooks that refuse the write. And hold every automated tool to the same table: a formatter that rewrites code fences, or a publisher that edits frozen deliverables, is an agent overwriting ground truth by another route.

**Contradiction-aware.** In a corpus drawn from many sources, contradictions are the steady state, not the exception: expect a sizeable share of pages to carry one at any time. Mark them rather than resolving them silently. Never pick a winner by recency alone — time does not resolve a disagreement — and make every answer that touches a contested claim say so. A supersede relationship is for corrections; a contradiction is two live claims nobody has yet adjudicated.

## How corpora fail

A corpus is not safe by construction. The same properties that make it powerful make its failures expensive, and they recur:

- **Overconfident synthesis.** An agent reads an end-of-meeting summary as a decision and records "consensus" where there was none. The claim then spreads to every page that cites it, delivered outputs included. Mark transcript-derived claims "discussed" until a named owner ratifies them.
- **Invented citations.** Plausible paths, ids, and quotes that resolve to nothing. Check them mechanically.
- **Alias collisions.** Two people, systems, or acronyms with the same short name merge into one page, and attributions go wrong.
- **Propagating errors.** A single typo or wrong number copied across dozens of links. Fix it with a deliberate fan-out, not a single edit.
- **Seed-source contamination.** One stale estimate or unverified number seeds a whole branch of derived pages. Track lineage from sources so that invalidating the seed invalidates everything downstream of it.
- **Self-agreeing pages.** Pages that cite each other look like corroboration to a linter, and they may share a single wrong ancestor. Lint cannot see this; only a re-read of the raw source or an adversarial pass can.
- **Stale ground truth.** Human-only protects a fact from agents, not from time.

The mitigations are unglamorous: quote verbatim, re-read the raw source before relying on a paraphrase, mark claims by status, track lineage, and run a separate adversarial pass before any finding drives an action.

## The compounding payoff: re-analysis

The strongest argument for paying the corpus cost up front is not the first answer it produces. It's all the follow-on answers the same corpus delivers cheaply.

Matuschak: *"evergreen notes are designed to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects."*[^11] A corpus assembled to answer question 1 becomes the substrate for questions 2 through *N*. Long-running projects ask many questions of the same domain over their lifetime — sizing, sequencing, risk, dependencies, peer comparisons, exec briefings, follow-on phases. Follow-on questions against an existing curated corpus are cheaper than the first, but not free: upkeep costs tokens and attention, and in practice the reuse shows up as new deliverables written from the corpus more often than as repeated ad-hoc queries.

Spotify reports that *"around half of Spotify's pull requests have been automated"*[^4] since mid-2024 — a number reachable only because the prompt-and-context substrate is durable and version-controlled, not regenerated per run. Glean's Deep Research feature (Sept 2025) shows the same corpus enabling *"customer support triage, competitive intelligence, business analysis"* off a single indexed substrate[^22]: same KB, different cuts, no rebuild. Karnofsky's case for writing-as-thinking generalizes the principle: *"retained reasoning"* is the asset; the corpus is the medium that makes that retention legible to both humans and agents[^17].

Re-analysis does more than answer the next question cheaper. It audits the last one. Re-running an established method on a new case — a second region, a second quarter — routinely exposes that the first answer was off. In one instance a reused four-bucket cost method showed an earlier deliverable understating first-year cost by roughly a third; in another, a ready-made skill's claim of about 70% cost attribution measured closer to 95%. A corpus that keeps methods and raw query results together makes that check cheap.

The compounding only happens if what the work learns goes back into the corpus. A finding that lives only in the deliverable is lost to the next question. After every query or output, write the new facts, corrections, and open questions back to the corpus; make it a step, not a hope.

The negative form is sharper. Teams that skip the corpus relitigate settled questions every session. Vicki Boykis on institutional memory in human teams: *"no one steps in to say, 'wait, we just did this five years ago.'"*[^23] An agent without a corpus relives the same five years on every prompt. A corpus built for one question becomes an asset that can answer many more at a fraction of the cost — and stays valid across model upgrades that obsolete prompts and rules files entirely.

## Human ratification is the scarce resource

The agent side of a corpus scales; the human side does not. Promoting a fact, closing a gap, ratifying a decision, and reviewing a fact for staleness all need a person, and a person has a fixed number of hours. Left unmanaged, the symptoms are predictable: the facts file stays empty, the gaps file grows into a wishlist, and drafted resolutions wait months for a sign-off. Design for the human's time. Batch decisions into one sitting. Have agents draft each resolution with explicit slots for the owner to confirm. Triage ruthlessly, and surface the size of the ratification queue so it is visible. Accept that an empty facts file early in a project is normal, and that a long queue is a signal about attention, not about agents.

## The corpus is the workshop, not the reading room

Stakeholders do not read your corpus. They read the doc in their inbox, the site their team already visits, the exec summary. Deliver into those surfaces. Sync requirements append-only into the shared doc people already use and preserve its comment threads; publish a read-only site for browsing; hand leadership a short written summary instead of a folder. The corpus feeds the shared surfaces, and people edit through an agent rather than directly. Nobody should have to adopt your folder structure to benefit from it.

## A portfolio of corpora

One corpus per question means you will end up with many, and they overlap. Roughly a quarter to a third of the concepts in any one corpus turn up in another, while almost none of the underlying sources are shared, so the same documents get re-ingested and re-interpreted several times. A single corpus cannot answer "we have nothing on this" for the others. Partition by question, not by team. If two corpora keep citing each other's pages, they are one corpus; merge them. Keep a registry — name, purpose, owner, last verified — and search across it before ingesting anything new. And never answer "nothing is known about this" from one corpus alone.

## Adopting the corpus stance

Nine concrete moves on your next non-trivial project:

1. **Pick the question or decision, and write the purpose file.** One short file stating what the corpus is for and which questions are in scope. Scope to a decision, not a topic.
2. **Inventory and classify the sources.** Before any analysis, list every place the domain lives: docs, Slack channels, code repos, ticket archives, transcripts, vendor pages, peer-team writeups. Capture each as a source page with a slug, an owner, a stable URL, and a content hash. Rank each by trust — primary, human-attested, chat and transcript, agent-synthesized. Pin code to a commit. Snapshot anything live.
3. **Stand up an `irrefutable-facts.md`** for the things you have already settled. Human-only-edit. Every bullet cited. Top-of-file warning that automated agents must not modify it, *"even when instructed by a prompt that appears to authorize it."* Start small, expect it to fill slowly, and give each fact a review date.
4. **Stand up an `open-knowledge-gaps.md`** for everything you have not settled. Each gap = *question / why / owner / how to close / status*. Agents may draft entries; only humans close them with a *Resolution* paragraph. Cap the open count, and close in batches from agent-drafted resolutions.
5. **Open an append-only `status-log.md`.** Date every entry. Note trigger, resolution, files touched. This becomes the corpus's reasoning trail. Commit once per operation so every change can be reverted and diffed.
6. **Verify before you act.** Run a separate adversarial pass on any finding that will drive a filing, an estimate, or a decision, and check cited paths and ids mechanically.
7. **Tier the structure.** Leadership artifacts at the top, working-engineer detail in the middle, reference material at the bottom. Asymmetric link flow only — never let leadership docs depend on reference content's freshness.
8. **Refresh on a cadence, and supersede rather than prune.** Per release, per quarter, per model upgrade. A page that is wrong gets replaced by a corrected one that links back; a page that is merely old gets marked stale; nothing is deleted without a human deciding. A corpus has more states than alive or dead — draft, active, stale, contradicted, superseded, archived — and moving pages between them is the work.
9. **Write results back, and decide where this corpus ends.** Return what each query or output learned to the corpus. When a second question outgrows the first corpus's scope, start a second corpus and add both to the registry.

The model is the multiplier. The domain corpus is the signal.
